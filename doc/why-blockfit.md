# Why I built BlockFit

I live in Brooklyn and like exercising outdoors, especially doing pull-ups. A gym is an option, but I enjoy being outside, and the exercise itself takes very little equipment. Finding that equipment has been the harder part. Friends and I have had trouble finding a suitable bar close enough to where we want to work out.

Around the Coney Island locations I use, pull-up bars are scarce, and good weather often brings a queue. What stuck with me was how a small piece of equipment could make outdoor exercise much more convenient.

I’ve actually used scaffolding for pull-ups because I couldn’t find a bar nearby. Anyone else? Finding somewhere to exercise shouldn’t require that much creativity.

## From finding a bar to finding a better place for one

My first idea was a more detailed way to find equipment. I wanted to complement existing parks information with answers to the questions I had when deciding where to go. Is there a pull-up bar? Where in the park is it? What do we know about whether it’s usable and publicly accessible? My own searches didn’t always get me to those answers, especially when I was looking for a specific kind of equipment.

That would be useful on its own. Someone who finds a bar today gets something out of the tool, even if the City never installs another one. But it also made me think about the opposite outcome. When people keep looking for something they can’t find, could they tell us what’s missing and where? A request would give a planning team a signal to consider alongside other ways of learning what a community needs.

Working at a nonprofit economic development organization serving New York City pushed me to think about the economics of that question. A government may already have a budget for a program. Given that budget, which location or combination might make the equipment available to more people who are currently underserved? Which options fit the goals of the program? A site that looks attractive on cost may raise practical concerns about schedule or disruption. I wanted a way to see those tradeoffs before a choice gets too far along.

Conversations with people who know economics helped me refine the idea. I also read publicly available material about how New York City collects community priorities and plans investments. The goal was to design something that could complement an existing process. I wanted staff to be able to inspect the assumptions, compare alternatives, and take a brief into a discussion they were already having.

## What comes next

I presented BlockFit at the November 2026 hackathon held by **NYCEDC (New York City Economic Development Corporation)**. The project didn’t receive the green light to move forward there. I’ve continued developing it independently, and the Brooklyn and Queens beta gives people something concrete to try and critique.

There’s plenty to learn. I’d like to hear from municipal staff, economists, researchers, practitioners, and potential collaborators about which evidence would make a comparison useful, where a tool like this would fit, and what would break first in a real pilot. I’d also like to hear from the person who just wants to find a pull-up bar. That is still where the project starts.
